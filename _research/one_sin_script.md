The One Sin the Bible Keeps Repeating

There is one sin the Bible tells four times. It happens first in a garden, then in the days before the flood, then at the fall of Jericho, and last on the roof of a king's palace. Each time, the Hebrew tells it with the same three words, in the same order: saw, good, took. But you see something good and take it every day, so what made it a sin those four times?

Here is the verse at the tree:

וַתֵּרֶא הָאִשָּׁה כִּי טוֹב הָעֵץ לְמַאֲכָל

"The woman saw that the tree was good for food." It ends:

וַתִּקַּח מִפִּרְיוֹ וַתֹּאכַל וַתִּתֵּן גַּם לְאִישָׁהּ עִמָּהּ וַיֹּאכַל

"She took of its fruit and ate, and gave also to her husband with her, and he ate."

Between the seeing and the taking, the verse says two more things about the tree:

וְכִי תַאֲוָה הוּא לָעֵינַיִם וְנֶחְמָד הָעֵץ לְהַשְׂכִּיל

"That it was a craving to the eyes, and that the tree was desirable for making one wise."

Only one other verse in the Bible puts craving and desire together. It's the last of the Ten Commandments, as Moses repeats them in Deuteronomy:

וְלֹא תַחְמֹד אֵשֶׁת רֵעֶךָ וְלֹא תִתְאַוֶּה בֵּית רֵעֶךָ

"And you shall not desire your neighbor's wife, and you shall not crave your neighbor's house." So the first thing a human takes in the Bible is told in the words of the last commandment.

Until that moment, only God had seen that something was good. Seven times in chapter one, he looks at what he has made, and every time it is good. In that same chapter, God says to the first humans, "I have given you every plant and every tree." In the garden he tells the man, "From every tree of the garden you may eat," and he holds back one.

In chapter three, for the first time, human eyes see that something is good, and a hand takes it.

Three chapters later, the same three words come back:

וַיִּרְאוּ בְנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים אֶת בְּנוֹת הָאָדָם כִּי טֹבֹת הֵנָּה וַיִּקְחוּ לָהֶם נָשִׁים מִכֹּל אֲשֶׁר בָּחָרוּ

"The sons of God saw the daughters of men, that they were good, and they took wives for themselves, whoever they chose." They saw, the women were good, and they took.

Three verses later, someone sees again, and this time it is God. "God saw that the evil of man was great on the earth." In chapter one, everything he saw was good. Now he looks and sees evil, and he says, "I will wipe out man from the face of the ground." And the flood comes.

But the flood does not end it. The same three words come back when Israel enters the promised land, in the mouth of a man named Achan.

At Jericho, Joshua warns the people that everything in the city belongs to God, and no one may take any of it. Achan takes from it anyway, and in the next battle, at a small town called Ai, about thirty-six men of Israel are killed. When Achan is found out, he confesses.

He begins:

וָאֵרֶא בַשָּׁלָל אַדֶּרֶת שִׁנְעָר אַחַת טוֹבָה

"I saw among the spoil one good robe from Shinar." He saw, and it was good.

He names silver and gold as well. Then he says:

וָאֶחְמְדֵם וָאֶקָּחֵם

"I desired them, and I took them."

"I desired" is the verb of the last commandment, and it is the word Genesis used for the tree. So Achan's confession has all of it: he saw, it was good, he desired, and he took.

But before the second attack on Ai, God says to Joshua, "Its spoil and its cattle you may plunder for yourselves." Achan stole spoil at Jericho, and one city later, spoil was given to all Israel.

Achan was one man, and about thirty-six died. The next man who sees and takes is the king.

By now, God has promised David a kingdom that will last forever. One evening, David walks on the roof of his palace:

וַיַּרְא אִשָּׁה רֹחֶצֶת מֵעַל הַגָּג

"He saw a woman bathing, from the roof."

וְהָאִשָּׁה טוֹבַת מַרְאֶה מְאֹד

"And the woman was very good to look at." She is the wife of Uriah, one of his own men, and two verses later comes:

וַיִּקָּחֶהָ

"He took her."

When the prophet Nathan comes to him, he first tells David about a rich man with huge flocks, who took a poor man's one little lamb. David says that man deserves to die, and Nathan says, "You are the man." Then God's message begins with what David was given. "I gave you your master's house. I gave you the house of Israel and Judah. And if that were too little, I would have added as much again." The charge that follows says, "his wife you took for yourself." First comes everything God gave him, and after it the one thing he took. This time the cost falls on his whole family: "The sword will never leave your house."

But the phrase from the tree comes back once more, and this time it is about a mother and her baby.

At the start of Exodus, Pharaoh orders every newborn son thrown into the Nile. In the next chapter, a woman from the tribe of Levi gives birth to a boy:

וַתֵּרֶא אֹתוֹ כִּי טוֹב הוּא

"She saw him, that he was good." The Bible says of a woman "she saw that it was good" only twice, at the tree and here.

At the tree, the next thing she does is take. Here the verse goes on:

וַתִּצְפְּנֵהוּ

"And she hid him." She hides him for three months, and when she can hide him no longer, she sets him in a basket among the reeds of the Nile.

Pharaoh's daughter finds him, and she hands him back to his own mother with the words, "Carry this child away and nurse him for me, and I will give you your wages." The verse goes on:

וַתִּקַּח הָאִשָּׁה הַיֶּלֶד

"The woman took the child." It is the verb from the tree, and she takes him only when he is handed to her.

The child is Moses. At the end of forty years in the desert, he is the man who stands at the Jordan and says, "You shall not desire, and you shall not crave."

Moses also tells Israel about a prayer of his own:

אֶעְבְּרָה נָּא וְאֶרְאֶה אֶת הָאָרֶץ הַטּוֹבָה

"Let me cross, please, and see the good land."

In the last chapter of the Torah, Moses goes up the mountain, and God shows him the whole land. God says, "This is the land I swore to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob: to your descendants I will give it." And he adds:

הֶרְאִיתִיךָ בְעֵינֶיךָ וְשָׁמָּה לֹא תַעֲבֹר

"I have let you see it with your eyes, but you will not cross over there." Moses asked to cross and to see. He never crosses. But he sees it, because God shows it to him.

So what made it a sin those four times? Each time, they helped themselves to something good, when the good was God's to give.

In the garden, he gave every tree but one. Before the flood, they took wives for themselves, but the first wife in the Bible was brought to the man by God. At Ai, he gave the spoil of the city. To David he said, "If that were too little, I would have added as much again." And on the mountain, he let Moses see the good land with his own eyes. From the first chapter of the Bible, God is the one who sees that something is good, and gives it.

One psalm takes the words from the tree and turns them around:

טַעֲמוּ וּרְאוּ כִּי טוֹב

"Taste and see that God is good." At the tree, she saw that the fruit was good, and she ate. The psalm says to taste, and to see, and the good is God himself.

The same psalm says, "Those who seek God will lack no good thing."
