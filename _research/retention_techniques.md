# Retention techniques for long-form scripts (research, Nov 22, 2026)

From the Garden of Eden session, where it was used for script 17 reviews (`inkofsinai-studio/work/reviews/17/retention_research.md`). Copied here so every session has it.

Each entry gives the term, what it is, the rule, and an example line. Words in [brackets] are slots, not facts. **NEW** means it isn't in `hooks.md` or `scripts_playbook.md`. **HAS** means the playbook already has it and the research adds a rule.

## Opening

**1. The three-part first 30 seconds (NEW as a frame).** *Rule:* 0–10 s shows one real instance that proves the title. 10–20 s gives the stake in the text, never a line about viewers. 20–30 s is one roadmap line that names the count or where the video is going. No greeting, no backstory, no table of contents. *Example:* "It happens [four] times. The fourth time, it happens to [the whole nation]."

**2. The hinge word (NEW piece; lean-in and snapback HAS).** Kallaway's hook runs lean-in, then a one-word interjection, then the snapback. *Rule:* start line 2 with the hinge ("But", "Then", "Except"). *Example:* "[He] waits [years] for a son. Then he's told to give him back."

## Loops

**3. A master loop plus rolling loops (NEW).** *Rule:* the title's question opens by 0:30 and closes at the end. Each smaller loop opens at the end of a section and closes within 2–4 minutes. Never keep more than two open. *Example:* "Each of these stories ends the same way, except one, and that one explains the rest."

**4. Nested loops, closed in reverse (NEW).** *Rule:* the last loop opened is the first one closed. Go only one level deep, because the viewer hears it once, and make the inner story mirror the outer one. *Example:* "The word comes back in the next story. But first, [she] has to say yes to a stranger."

**5. The handoff (NEW).** *Rule:* at every section transition, the sentence that pays off one section also opens the next. Mid-video drop-off clusters at transitions. *Example:* "So [the younger brother] gets the blessing. And [years] later, he's the one being tricked."

**6. The unflagged plant (NEW).** *Rule:* a setup works on its own, carries a concrete image and is never flagged ("remember this"). The payoff is what makes it matter. *Example:* "[He] rolls [the stone] back over the mouth of the well."

## Middle

**7. Progressive information (NEW).** Mario Joos's test: pacing is the speed of new information, not cutting speed. *Rule:* each line either moves the argument (a new fact, verse or instance) or doesn't (a reaction, a recap, an aside). One reaction after a peak is fine; two lines in a row that don't move the argument cause a dip. *Keep:* "The same verb returns, and this time its subject is God." *Cut:* "Which is fascinating when you stop and think about it."

**8. The mode switch (NEW for scripts).** *Rule:* every 60–90 s, change what the narration is doing: a scene, a verse, a count, the human cost, a question carried forward. Three retellings in a row read flat however good each line is. *Example:* "Stay on one word in that verse."

**9. The 3:00 re-engagement (HAS, plus MrBeast's memo).** *Rule:* near minute 3 comes the most striking reveal so far, one that fits the story, never a recap. *Example:* "Until now, every [mother] in this pattern asks for a child. The next one never asks."

**10. The midpoint false resolution (NEW).** *Rule:* around the middle, state the obvious reading fairly and let it hold for a beat, then show the case it can't explain. The viewer should feel outsmarted, not tricked. *Example:* "So it's about [younger sons]. That reading holds for [three] stories. In the [fourth], the younger son loses everything."

**11. The strongest instance at two-thirds (NEW).** *Rule:* most viewers never reach the end, so the most striking instance goes at about 60–70% of the runtime. The God layer stays as the final payoff. *Example:* "The next one is the strangest, and it's why the pattern stops where it does."

**12. The dip checklist (NEW).** An early drop means the hook failed. A slow slide means a pacing problem. A cliff means a missing bridge. A dip followed by a spike means viewers skipped that stretch, so cut it. *Script causes:* a context dump before the first proof, a "before we start" detour, a jump with no but/therefore, a second proof of a point already made, a term with nothing to anchor it, and a line that sounds like an ending. *Bridge:* "That's the pattern in one family. Therefore it returns when the family becomes a nation."

## Ending

**13. A callback that changes meaning (NEW nuance; callback HAS).** *Rule:* the last line brings back the exact opening image in a new context. Reversing its terms marks the shift, and the line still makes sense to someone who forgot the opening. *Example:* the open "It starts with [one woman] at a well, alone." becomes the close "It ends with [a whole nation] at a well, singing."

**14. Re-hook templates.** A pointer with a cost: "[The next brother] gets the same promise. It costs him [his name]." A climb: "So far it's been families. Next, it's [a nation]." A break: "Every time until now, [X]. This time, [the opposite]."

**Confirmed, already in the playbook:** but/therefore, curiosity at about 50% certainty, the 3:00 and 6:00 re-hooks, the stakes ladder, restating the question before the payoff, slowing down before the reveal, and ending fast.
