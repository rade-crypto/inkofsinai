# 06 · David and Bathsheba: The One Word That Tells the Whole Crime. Voiceover, draft 1

**Title:** David and Bathsheba: The One Word That Tells the Whole Crime
**Thumbnail:** David on the palace roof at night, in gold ink. Glowing circle on a messenger's sealed letter. Text: "HE **SENT.**"

---

King David takes another man's wife and has the man killed, and he never leaves his palace to do it.

He doesn't walk to her door. He doesn't go near the battle. Every move goes out through one Hebrew word: שָׁלַח. Send.

In 2 Samuel 11, that word appears twelve times. No chapter in the Bible uses it more.

Follow it, and you can watch the crime happen one message at a time. And when the twelfth message lands, the word changes hands.

It starts in verse 1. Spring, "the time when kings go out" to war. David sends Joab and the whole army. But David stays in Jerusalem.

Even that line hides the word in its letters. It's read "kings," מְלָכִים. But the letters on the page add one silent letter, an aleph: מַלְאָכִים. Messengers. As it's written, this chapter opens at the time when messengers go out.

Then the scene everyone knows. Some say Bathsheba bathed on a rooftop to be seen. But read the verse. David gets up from his bed in the evening and walks on the roof of the king's house, and from the roof he sees a woman bathing. The only roof in that verse is his.

And he doesn't go to her. He sends. David sends to ask who she is, and the answer names her husband: "Bathsheba, daughter of Eliam, wife of Uriah the Hittite."

So now he knows she's married. But he sends again. וַיִּשְׁלַח דָּוִד מַלְאָכִים וַיִּקָּחֶהָ. "David sent messengers, and took her," and lay with her.

She goes home. Then she sends something back. Two words: הָרָה אָנֹכִי. "I'm pregnant." In 2 Samuel, those are the only words she ever says, and they arrive by messenger.

Now David needs her husband home, fast. One verse, three sends. David sends to Joab: שְׁלַח אֵלַי אֶת אוּרִיָּה, "send me Uriah." And Joab sends Uriah.

The plan is simple. If Uriah sleeps at home, the baby will pass as his. So David tells him, "Go down to your house and wash your feet."

But Uriah sleeps at the palace door, with the servants. David asks him why, and Uriah answers with an oath: "The ark, and Israel, and Judah are living in tents. My master Joab and his men are camped in the open field. And I should go to my house לֶאֱכֹל וְלִשְׁתּוֹת וְלִשְׁכַּב עִם אִשְׁתִּי, to eat, and to drink, and to lie with my wife?"

Eat. Drink. Lie with my wife. A prophet is going to hand those three words back to David.

So David tries once more. He keeps Uriah another day, has him eat and drink at his table, and gets him drunk. Two out of three. But that night Uriah sleeps with the servants again. He never goes home.

So David sends a different kind of message.

In the morning he writes a letter to Joab, וַיִּשְׁלַח בְּיַד אוּרִיָּה, and sends it by the hand of Uriah. Uriah carries his own death order back to the front.

The letter says: "Put Uriah in front, where the fighting is fiercest. Then pull back from him, so that he is struck and dies."

Joab does it. Uriah dies, and other soldiers with him. Joab sends a messenger with the report, and coaches him: if the king gets angry about the losses, add one line. "Your servant Uriah the Hittite is dead too."

The messenger says it. And David gives him an answer for Joab: אַל יֵרַע בְּעֵינֶיךָ אֶת הַדָּבָר הַזֶּה. "Don't let this thing be evil in your eyes. The sword devours this one and that one."

Bathsheba mourns her husband. When the mourning is over, David sends one last time and brings her into his house. She becomes his wife and bears him a son.

That's twelve. Count who held the word. Eight belong to David. Three to Joab. One to Bathsheba, for her two words. None to Uriah. Uriah is the one who gets sent.

And the chapter's last line answers David's message to Joab. He had said, don't let this be evil in your eyes. The narrator says, "But the thing David had done was evil in the eyes of God."

Then the very next verse, the first of chapter 12. For the first time in this story, the sender is someone new. "And God sent Nathan to David."

Nathan doesn't accuse him. He brings the king a case to judge. Two men in one city, one rich, one poor. The rich man has flocks and herds. The poor man has nothing but one little ewe lamb. He raised it with his children. מִפִּתּוֹ תֹאכַל וּמִכֹּסוֹ תִשְׁתֶּה וּבְחֵיקוֹ תִשְׁכָּב. "It ate from his bread, drank from his cup, and lay in his arms."

Eat. Drink. Lie. Uriah's three verbs, in the same order. The lamb lives the life Uriah wouldn't let himself have.

A traveler comes to the rich man. He won't take from his own flock. He takes the poor man's lamb and serves it.

David explodes. "The man who did this is בֶן מָוֶת, a son of death. He must repay the lamb four times over."

And Nathan says two words: אַתָּה הָאִישׁ. "You are the man."

Then the verdict, and it starts from David's own line about the sword. "You struck Uriah the Hittite with the sword. You took his wife. You killed him with the sword of the Ammonites. So now, לֹא תָסוּר חֶרֶב מִבֵּיתְךָ עַד עוֹלָם, the sword will never leave your house."

David answers with two words in Hebrew: "I have sinned against God."

Two words from Bathsheba started the cover-up. Two words from David end it.
